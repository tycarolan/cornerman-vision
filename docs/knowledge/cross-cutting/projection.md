# Projection — what a single 2D camera cannot see

> **Authoritative for**: the geometric constraint underlying the rear-hand, scale-reference, and elbow-angle failures; what the system may and may not claim from 2D data.
> **Related**: `../domains/trace-format.md`, `../processes/capture-protocol.md`
> **Last verified**: 2026-07-28

## Rules

**A single 2D camera cannot see motion travelling along its own depth axis.** This is the one binding constraint the whole pipeline works around, and it is geometric, not a tuning problem — no better 2D model, no threshold adjustment, and no amount of device performance fixes it (spec, Pose inference). Every failure below is this same fact showing up in a different stage of the pipeline.

**At roughly 45°, the rear hand is the one aligned with the depth axis, and its punches are undetectable rather than merely harder to detect.** Measured over a 20-second round: the rear hand was tracked in 91% of frames — present, confident, not occluded — yet its 99th-percentile normalized reach was 0.86 against the lead hand's 1.61, and it crossed the detection threshold in 6 frames against the lead hand's 101. Zero rear-hand punches were detected while crosses were being thrown. Lowering the threshold does not recover them: at 0.9 the lead hand still has 183 qualifying frames to the rear hand's 8 (spec, Setup and framing). The signal is absent, not weak — it was never projected into the image, so no amount of threshold tuning finds it.

**The 2D scale reference foreshortens at oblique angles, which inflates every reach value it normalizes.** Shoulder width is the denominator for normalized reach, and at exactly the angles this spec recommends, shoulder width shrinks in the projected image while the true 3D distance does not. Measured peak reach reached 1.84 shoulder-widths, which is geometrically impossible — wrist-to-shoulder distance cannot exceed upper arm plus forearm, roughly 1.6 (spec, Normalization). The consequence is that thresholds tuned at one camera placement do not transfer to another, which directly contradicts acceptance criteria 3 and 4 (spec, Normalization; Acceptance criteria).

**Elbow angle at peak is unusable in 2D, for two independent projection-caused reasons.** Across ten detected punches, every single one peaked at an elbow angle between 74° and 95° — read as bent, when straights were among what was thrown. Meanwhile 111 frames read above 140°, and none fell within 0.35s of a detected punch: that is the arm hanging relaxed at the side, not a punch. The two causes are foreshortening — an arm extended toward the camera projects as a bent arm — and keypoint drift under speed, since the elbow is the fastest-moving and most frequently occluded of the three tracked joints, while wrist and shoulder remain reliable (spec, Punch classification).

**A synthetic check confirms the mechanism exactly, and shows where 2D and 3D agree.** For an arm extended straight along the camera axis, the 2D metric reports reach 0.0 and elbow angle 90°, while the 3D metric on the same landmarks reports reach 1.5 and elbow angle 180°. When motion lies in the image plane instead, the two metrics agree precisely (spec, Pose inference). Projection error is not a constant penalty on every measurement — it is zero when motion is lateral or vertical to the camera and total when motion is directly toward or away from it.

**The system must never report a number whose underlying signal was never projected into the image, and must state plainly which hand a given claim covers.** This follows directly from the rear-hand finding above: a metric computed from an absent signal is not a low-confidence estimate, it is a fabrication with a confidence score attached. It is why acceptance criterion 11 requires six-way classification confidence to be surfaced rather than asserted, why criterion 13 makes rear-hand recall the criterion that decides whether six-punch reporting ships at all, and why the spec elsewhere insists that thermal degradation be surfaced honestly rather than silently corrupting the numbers (spec, Acceptance criteria; Performance and thermals).

## Patterns

**An oblique setup does not make both hands visible — it trades which hand is legible.** Punching straight at the camera puts the punching hand on the depth axis; side-on flattens hooks into ambiguity; 45° puts the *rear* hand on the depth axis instead of removing the problem. There is no 2D camera angle at which both hands are simultaneously well-projected, because the two hands are on opposite sides of the body and only one side of the body can be angled away from the depth axis at a time (spec, Setup and framing).

**2D and 3D must be measured on the same frames, never on separate runs.** The only way to settle whether depth actually recovers a 2D failure is to compute both metrics from identical keypoints at identical instants — a difference measured across two separate captures could just as easily come from throw-to-throw variation as from the model. This is why the trace format's per-frame side objects always carry both `reach`/`elbow` (depth-aware) and `reach2d`/`elbow2d` (always projected) on the same sample, falling back to identical pairs when no depth is available (`../domains/trace-format.md`).

**If depth does not recover the rear hand, the product narrows rather than ships an inaccurate result.** Phases 3 and 4 — six-way classification and the learned classifier — are dropped, not shipped in a degraded form, and the product settles on work rate and guard tracking instead (spec, Why this, and why it's scoped this way; Phasing). Guard tracking is deliberately exempt from all of the above: the non-punching hand is slow and nearly static, which is exactly the condition pose models handle reliably, and it is the opposite of the fast, depth-crossing motion that causes every failure in this document.

## History

**The spec originally treated framerate as the binding constraint, and it was wrong by roughly 2×.** The scoping assumed 30fps against a 100–150ms jab, yielding four or five frames per punch — tight enough that frame budget appeared to be what shaped every downstream decision, including ruling out BlazePose in favor of MoveNet Lightning on speed grounds alone. Phase 0 measured 57.9fps sustained on the target device, with 5.7 mean frames per punch on 98ms punches — comfortably above the four-frame floor the spec had set as the point past which classification would be abandoned (spec, Why this, and why it's scoped this way; Phasing).

**Correcting the framerate figure did not remove the constraint — it revealed the real one underneath it.** Once frame budget stopped being the bottleneck, Phase 0's traces surfaced the rear-hand, scale-reference, and elbow-angle failures above, all traceable to the same geometric fact: projection along the depth axis. This constraint is harder to work around than framerate ever was, because framerate is a device-performance problem that faster hardware or a lighter model can shrink, while projection is a fact about a single 2D camera that no amount of device performance touches (spec, Why this, and why it's scoped this way; Pose inference).

**This is why the model decision was reopened rather than left settled.** MoveNet Lightning was chosen on the strength of the (wrong) 30fps ceiling that ruled out BlazePose as too slow. With that ceiling corrected, the argument for the lighter model no longer holds on its own, and BlazePose's 33 landmarks with a depth coordinate are the only known answer to the rear-hand and elbow-angle failures above. The trade is now temporal resolution against dimensional completeness, to be settled by measurement in a gym session rather than by the armchair reasoning that produced the original choice (spec, Pose inference).
