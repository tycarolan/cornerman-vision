CORNERMAN VISION — MOCKUP CONCEPTS

The phone watches the round.

Prop it at 45°, hit start, and work. It counts what you threw, how hard you kept working, and — the part nothing else does well — whether your guard stayed up. Six screens, and the reasoning behind each.

01

You cannot look at the phone

You're eight feet away, moving, hands up. The live screen gets glanced at between combos for half a second. Anything that needs reading is wasted pixels.

02

Live feedback is audio

Guard drops are corrected in the moment or not at all — and you won't see a visual cue mid-combo. A tick in your ear is the only channel that works while you're working.

03

Detail lives in the rest period

Sixty seconds between rounds is the only time you're actually looking at the screen. That's where numbers, deltas, and charts belong — not during the work.

04

Never claim more than it knows

Count and guard position are reliable. Punch type is shaky at speed. Technique grading is a lie. The UI shows confidence honestly rather than inventing precision.

SCREEN 01

Framing

The single highest-leverage screen in the app, and the one most products skip. Bad framing produces bad data silently — you'd never know the numbers were garbage. So the app refuses to start until it's happy.

A dashed silhouette shows where to stand. Four live checks — distance, angle, light, full body in frame — each pass independently. The start button stays dead until all four are green, which is deliberately a little annoying.

The corner diagram is doing real work: it shows the phone's field of view and your body at 45° to it, because "stand at an angle" is genuinely hard to interpret from words alone.

WHY 45°

Punching straight at the camera is the natural setup and the worst possible one — a jab travels almost entirely toward the lens, which a 2D-dominant model barely registers as motion. Side-on nails straights but flattens hooks. Forty-five degrees is the only angle where both have a visible component.

SCREEN 02 / 03

The live round — guard up, guard down

Three numbers. That's the whole screen. Time left, punches thrown, punches per minute. It has to be legible from eight feet in a half-second glance while you're breathing hard, which means enormous type and nothing competing with it.

The interesting move is guard state as a screen-edge glow rather than a widget. A red border filling your peripheral vision registers while you're mid-combo and looking somewhere else entirely. A number would not. Green edge is a faint reassurance; red is impossible to miss.

The docked skeleton bottom-right is small on purpose. Its only job is answering "is this thing actually tracking me?" — a question you'll have constantly, and one that's corrosive if unanswerable. It turns red with the guard state too.

THE REAL CHANNEL IS AUDIO

Every visual here is secondary. A guard drop should fire a short tick in your ear the instant it happens — that's the only feedback that reaches you while your hands are up and your eyes are on an imaginary opponent. The screen is for the glance between combos and the review afterward.

SCREEN 04

Round summary — the rest period

The sixty seconds between rounds is the only window where you're genuinely looking at the phone, catching your breath. So this is where every number lives, and it's dense on purpose — the opposite of the live screen.

Every metric carries a delta against the previous round, because the interesting question is never "how many punches" — it's "am I falling off?" A raw count is trivia; a −7 on guard discipline is a coaching cue you can act on in the next three minutes.

The output curve is the most honest thing on the screen. It shows exactly where in the round you faded, which is the kind of thing you feel vaguely and never quantify.

"WORKING %" EARNS ITS PLACE

Gross motion detection is nearly free and needs no ML at all — it's the one metric that's trivially reliable. It catches the honest failure mode nobody logs: standing around between combos. Cheap to compute, uncomfortable to read, which is what makes it useful.

SCREEN 05

Session summary

One hero number, six bars, two averages, and whatever record you just broke. The bar chart is the whole story — you can see at a glance that round 3 was the peak and everything after it was decline.

Personal bests are the replay hook, and they're the same competitive-against-yourself mechanic you asked for in a game. Here it comes free, because the data's already being collected. No leaderboard, no social layer, no accounts — the only opponent is last Tuesday.

THIS IS WHERE THE GAME WENT

The score-attack idea survived — it just moved out of a fake tapping game and into the real workout. Beating 604 punches means something because you actually threw them. That's a better version of the thing Mitts was reaching for.

SCREEN 06

Guard timeline — the differentiator

This is the screen that justifies the whole product, and it's built on the easiest thing to detect rather than the hardest. Your non-punching hand is slow and nearly static — precisely what pose models are reliable at, the exact opposite of the fast-hand problem that makes punch classification shaky.

Each bar is a round; each red mark is a stretch where the guard was down. Stacked, they reveal something you'd never notice from inside the workout: the drops migrate earlier as you fatigue. That's a genuine coaching insight, and it's the kind of pattern that only exists across a whole session.

EASY AND VALUABLE, NOT HARD AND FLASHY

Punch classification is the feature everyone would demo. Guard tracking is the one that's both achievable today in a browser and actually worth having. Build the reliable thing that helps rather than the impressive thing that's wrong 30% of the time.

Mockups only — nothing here is implemented, and every number is invented. Feasibility still hinges on one unanswered question: whether punch events are cleanly separable from pose data at your speed, in your lighting. That's the spike, and it comes before any of this.
